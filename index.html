Acute Cholecystitis and Cholangitis
🟢 Stable 🟡 Urgent 🔴 Immediate
When to call SpR
🟢 Stable cholecystitis
🟡 Any cholangitis
🔴 Reynolds' pentad / peritonism / sepsis

Cholecystitis = gallbladder wall inflammation.
Cholangitis = infected bile duct with up to 20% mortality untreated.
Both commonly arise from gallstones, but cholangitis can also be caused by malignancy, bile duct strictures, biliary stents, or recent ERCP.
The key distinction is Charcot's triad and haemodynamic status — these determine how fast you act.

📋H · E · I · M — Summary
HistoryRUQ pain, fever, nausea. Jaundice = cholangitis. Biliary history, alcohol, medications.
ExamTemperature, HR, BP. Murphy's sign. Scleral icterus. Peritonism = perforated → escalate.
InvFBC, CRP, lactate, U&E, LFTs, amylase, clotting, G&S. INR (cholangitis). Blood cultures x2. USS abdomen.
ManagementNBM · IV fluids · IV paracetamol ± PR diclofenac ± morphine · anti-emetics · co-amoxiclav 1.2g TDS (UHDB)
Expand for detail
H History
  • Antibiotics — UHDB:
    • <35 years: Ceftriaxone 1g IM/IV single dose + doxycycline 100mg BD ×10 days
    • ≥35 years: ciprofloxacin 500mg BD ×10 days
    • Triple antibiotics per UHDB trust guidelines (check NETI for current regimen — typically broad-spectrum + anaerobic + Gram-positive cover; older regimen was meropenem 1g IV TDS + doxycycline 100mg BD).
  • RUQ pain — onset, duration, radiation to right shoulder tip
  • Fever, rigors, nausea, vomiting
  • Jaundice (skin, eyes, dark urine, pale stools) — if present, suspect cholangitis
  • Previous biliary history, gallstones, prior ERCP or biliary surgery
  • Alcohol history, medications, PMH of liver disease

Common causes

SBO (small bowel)LBO (large bowel)
Adhesions (most common)Colorectal cancer (most common)
Herniae (examine all orifices)Sigmoid / caecal volvulus
Crohn's strictureDiverticular stricture
Malignancy, intussusceptionFaecal impaction, pseudo-obstruction
E Examination
  • Temperature, HR, BP, RR — any sepsis features?
  • Murphy's sign: two fingers pressed in right subcostal margin, ask patient to breathe in — inspiratory arrest = positive (specificity 79–96%, poor sensitivity — negative sign does NOT exclude cholecystitis, especially in the elderly)
  • Scleral icterus, jaundice — suggests cholangitis
  • Generalised peritonism, rigidity, sudden worsening = perforated cholecystitis → call SpR immediately
  • Charcot's triad (only 30% of cholangitis): RUQ pain + fever + jaundice
  • Reynolds' pentad (Charcot's triad + confusion + hypotension) suggests severe cholangitis. Note: Grade III cholangitis is defined by organ dysfunction, not Reynolds' pentad alone — these may not always coincide.
I Investigations
  • Bloods: FBC, CRP, lactate, U&E, LFTs (ALP/GGT/bilirubin/ALT), amylase, clotting, G&S. Add INR for cholangitis.
  • Blood cultures x2 — from 2 separate sites, 1–15 minutes apart — before starting antibiotics
  • Urine dip (exclude pyelonephritis); pregnancy test if female of childbearing age
  • USS abdomen — first-line: gallstones, GB wall thickness (>4mm), pericholecystic fluid, CBD diameter. A dilated CBD supports biliary obstruction, but interpret with clinical picture, LFTs, age, and previous cholecystectomy status.
  • CT if USS inconclusive, complicated picture, or perforation suspected
M Management — F1 Actions
  1. ERCP pre-medications (RDH): follow current RDH/UHDB ERCP protocol. Check renal function and NSAID contraindications before PR diclofenac — confirm with endoscopy team.
  2. NBM, IV fluids, strict fluid balance
  3. Analgesia: paracetamol 1g IV ± PR diclofenac 75mg (avoid if eGFR <30) ± morphine IV titrated
  4. Anti-emetics: ondansetron or cyclizine
  5. IV antibiotics — community acquired, stable: co-amoxiclav 1.2g 8-hourly. Unstable/septic/hospital-acquired: pip-tazo 4.5g 8-hourly. See Antibiotic Quick Reference for allergy alternatives.
  6. Enoxaparin + compression (TED) stockings
  7. Monitor observations — escalate if sepsis develops
👨‍⚕️Seniors Will
  • Grade cholecystitis and plan early or same-admission laparoscopic cholecystectomy — NICE recommends within 1 week of diagnosis where possible. Timing follows local hot gallbladder pathway.
  • Grade cholangitis and plan biliary drainage: Grade I = antibiotics ± ERCP if no response; Grade II = early ERCP 24–48hrs; Grade III (Reynolds' pentad) = emergency ERCP + ICU
  • Decide cholecystostomy vs cholecystectomy for acalculous cases and perforated cholecystitis

🚨 Escalate Immediately If

  • Reynolds' pentad — Grade III cholangitis
  • Peritonism or sudden worsening pain — perforated cholecystitis
  • Any suspected cholangitis — discuss with SpR early, even if stable, because urgent ERCP or biliary drainage may be needed
  • Failure to improve in 24hrs on IV antibiotics
  • Sepsis features

💡 Pearls

  • Charcot's triad present in only 30% — absence does NOT exclude cholangitis
  • Hospital-acquired infections: pip-tazo 4.5g every 6 hours (not 8 hourly)
Acute Pancreatitis
🟢 Stable 🟡 Urgent 🔴 Immediate
When to call SpR
🟢 Mild
🟡 Glasgow approaching 3 at 48hrs
🔴 Organ failure / lactate >2 / Glasgow ≥3

80% mild and self-limiting.
Aggressive fluid resuscitation is the most important F1 action.
No prophylactic antibiotics.

📋H · E · I · M — Summary
HistoryEpigastric pain radiating to back, relieved by leaning forward. Nausea, vomiting. Alcohol history. Gallstones.
ExamTemperature, HR, BP, SpO2. Epigastric tenderness. Grey Turner's/Cullen's = severe/retroperitoneal haemorrhage.
InvFBC, U&E, LFTs, amylase/lipase, calcium, glucose, CRP, clotting. VBG. USS within 24hrs. CT day 5 only (UHDB).
ManagementOral diet if tolerated · urinary catheter · Hartmann's IV aggressive (250–500ml/hr) · IV paracetamol + morphine · no prophylactic antibiotics (BSG)
Expand for detail
H History
  • Epigastric pain — onset, severity, radiation to the back, relieved by leaning forward
  • Nausea, vomiting, anorexia
  • Cause: alcohol history (amount, recent binge); gallstone history; recent ERCP; medications (check BNF); hypertriglyceridaemia history
  • Previous episodes — recurrent pancreatitis changes management
  • Duration of symptoms — delay before presentation influences severity assessment
E Examination
  • Temperature, HR, BP, RR, SpO2 — organ failure markers
  • Epigastric tenderness — extent of guarding indicates severity
  • Grey Turner's sign (flank bruising) and Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising) — retroperitoneal haemorrhage, late and severe signs
  • SpO2 — respiratory failure is a severity criterion
  • Jaundice — suggests biliary obstruction (gallstone aetiology; consider urgent ERCP if CBD occluded)
I Investigations
  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, LFTs (ALT >3x ULN suggests gallstone cause), amylase or lipase (lipase preferred — amylase non-specific), calcium, glucose, CRP, clotting. Repeat bloods at 24hrs and 48hrs for Glasgow scoring (Glasgow mnemonic: PANCREAS — PaO₂, Age, Neutrophils, Calcium, Renal/Urea, Enzymes/LDH, Albumin, Sugar/Glucose).
  • VBG — blood glucose, lactate
  • Diagnosis: 2 of 3 criteria: characteristic pain + lipase/amylase >3x upper limit of normal + CT findings
  • USS within 24hrs: gallstones? CBD dilatation (>6mm)? Liver appearances?
  • CT abdomen: do NOT perform on admission to establish diagnosis or as baseline. Optimal timing is day 5 in moderate/severe disease or if failing to improve. (UHDB guideline)
  • ERCP: only if stone is occluding the bile duct causing jaundice — discuss with gastroenterology
M Management — F1 Actions
  1. No need for routine NBM (UHDB guideline) — oral diet can continue if tolerated.
  2. Urinary catheter — essential for fluid balance.
  3. Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation: Hartmann's preferred over 0.9% saline (evidence: reduced SIRS at 24hrs). Start at 250–500ml/hr as a guide — adjust to clinical response and per UHDB guideline. Target urine output (UO) ≥0.5ml/kg/hr. Do NOT over-resuscitate — abdominal compartment syndrome risk in severe disease.
  4. IV paracetamol 1g QDS + IV morphine for pain. Anti-emetics.
  5. Enoxaparin + TED stockings.
  6. No prophylactic antibiotics (BSG + UHDB guideline) — only if confirmed infected necrosis, cholangitis, or sepsis from unclear source.
  7. Refer to critical care outreach if NEWS ≥3 in a single parameter or total NEWS ≥5 (UHDB guideline).
👨‍⚕️Seniors Will
  • Assess Glasgow score at 48hrs and manage severity
  • Gallstone pancreatitis: laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 2 weeks of recovery (NICE). Consider hot lap chole on index admission.
  • Complex/severe pancreatitis: refer to QMC HPB — registrar 07812 268274 or referapatient.org. UHDB has no on-call HPB team.
  • Restart oral diet as tolerated — early oral feeding is safe in mild disease

🚨 Escalate Immediately If

  • Glasgow ≥3 at 48hrs
  • CRP >150 at 48hrs
  • Organ failure (oliguria, SpO2 falling, hypotension)
  • Lactate >2
  • No improvement at 48hrs

💡 Pearls

  • No prophylactic antibiotics — actively harmful (BSG + UHDB guideline)
  • CT on day 5, not on admission — premature CT underestimates severity
  • UHDB has no on-call HPB team — tertiary referral to QMC for complex cases
Upper GI Bleed
🟢 Stable 🟡 Urgent 🔴 Immediate
When to call SpR
🟢 Glasgow-Blatchford score 0, stable
🟡 Active bleeding, stable
🔴 Haemodynamic instability / activate MHP or melaena. Tachycardia develops before hypotension — act before the BP drops.

Bleeding from the oesophagus, stomach, or duodenum — presenting as haematemesis (fresh red or coffee-ground vomit) or melaena. Common causes: peptic ulcer disease, oesophageal varices, and Mallory-Weiss tears. Resuscitation comes before endoscopy — assess haemodynamic status first and risk-stratify with the Glasgow-Blatchford score.

📋H · E · I · M — Summary
HistoryHaematemesis (fresh red = brisk; coffee-ground = slow) or melaena. NSAIDs, anticoagulants, alcohol, liver disease.
ExamHR + BP. Signs of chronic liver disease. Pallor. Tachycardia comes before hypotension.
InvVBG (Hb + lactate). FBC, U&E, LFTs, clotting, G&S, crossmatch. GBS on Endoscopy Request Form (UHDB).
ManagementTwo large-bore cannulae · sip till send (not NBM) · monitor continuously · MHP if massive · transfuse if Hb <70
Expand for detail
H History
  • Haematemesis: fresh red blood (brisk bleeding) or coffee-ground vomit (slower, partially digested)
  • Melaena: black, tarry, offensive stool — strongly suggests upper GI source
  • Amount and frequency of bleeding
  • Risk factors: NSAIDs, anticoagulants, aspirin, alcohol, known liver disease or varices, H. pylori, previous UGI bleed, peptic ulcer history
  • Symptoms suggesting varices: known cirrhosis, portal hypertension, previous variceal bleed, abdominal distension (ascites)
  • Syncope, dizziness, palpitations — markers of significant haemodynamic compromise
E Examination
  • HR and BP (lying and standing) — tachycardia is often the earliest sign
  • Signs of chronic liver disease: jaundice, spider naevi, caput medusae, ascites, splenomegaly, palmar erythema — if present → start variceal protocol
  • Pallor, capillary refill
  • Abdominal examination — epigastric tenderness (peptic ulcer), hepatosplenomegaly
I Investigations
  • VBG immediately — Hb and lactate at the bedside. Hb on VBG is your earliest guide.
  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, LFTs, clotting, G&S, crossmatch
  • Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS) — use the Endoscopy Request Form pre-endoscopy. GBS 0 = consider discharge + outpatient endoscopy. GBS ≥1 = admit. GBS 0–1 with no exclusion criteria → refer to medical SDEC (Ward 102 RDH) with follow-up appointment.
M Management — F1 Actions
  1. A–E. Two large-bore cannulae.
  2. Not nil by mouth (UHDB) — "sip till send". Allow clear fluids and medications before gastroscopy.
  3. Monitor HR, BP, urine output, ongoing bleeding continuously. Document all episodes.
  4. If massive or unstable → activate MHP
  5. Transfuse if shocked or Hb <70 g/L — aim Hb 70–100 g/L. No transfusion if no shock and no anaemia.
  6. Do NOT use Tranexamic Acid | Do NOT start PPI before gastroscopy
👨‍⚕️Seniors Will
  • Request endoscopy on Lorenzo (RDH) — daily inpatient list 7 days/week, allocated at 10am. Use GBS on Endoscopy Request Form.
  • Use post-endoscopy Rockall Score to guide further management
  • Platelets if actively bleeding and platelet count <50 (senior decision)
  • FFP if INR >1.5 (senior decision)
  • Warfarin + active bleeding: vitamin K 10mg IV + Octoplex (Prothrombin Complex Concentrate) — senior/haematology decision. DOACs: see intranet guideline.
  • If liver disease or known varices: IV co-amoxiclav 1.2g TDS (allergy: ceftriaxone then oral ciprofloxacin; severe allergy: IV/oral ciprofloxacin) + terlipressin 2mg QDS first dose pre-endoscopy (contraindicated: ischaemic ECG, IHD, ischaemic stroke, PVD; caution diabetes/>75yrs — use octreotide 100mcg bolus then 50mcg/hr x48hrs instead)
  • Patient unstable despite resuscitation → discuss with on-call gastroenterologist (available 24/7 at RDH) for emergency gastroscopy

🚨 Escalate Immediately If

  • Haemodynamic instability — activate MHP
  • Ongoing haematemesis with falling Hb
  • Patient unstable despite resuscitation

💡 Pearls

  • Tachycardia comes before hypotension — do not wait for a low BP to act
  • Melaena = upper GI until proven otherwise
  • No TXA, no pre-scope PPI, not routinely NBM — these are common errors to avoid
Bowel Obstruction
🟢 Stable 🟡 Urgent 🔴 Immediate
When to call SpR
🟢 Adhesional SBO, no ischaemia
🟡 LBO, no deterioration
🔴 Lactate >2 / peritonism / closed-loop / caecum >10cm

SBO (adhesions most common) or LBO (colorectal cancer most common).
The critical questions every time are: is there strangulation? and is there a closed loop?
Raised lactate and peritonism change this to an immediate emergency.

H History
  • Sudden onset severe abdominal pain — often described as "like being stabbed" or "winded"

  • Shoulder tip pain — diaphragmatic irritation from free gas

  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, CRP, amylase, clotting, G&S. Blood cultures x2. VBG — lactate.

  • Haemodynamic instability
  • Hinchey III/IV

Strangulated Hernia
🔴 Immediate
When to call SpR
Call SpR at time of assessment for any irreducible tender hernia — bowel necrosis occurs within hours.
🟢 Irreducible but soft, no obstruction or compromise
🟡 Obstructed (vomiting, distension, absolute constipation)
🔴 Ischaemic — skin changes over sac, rising lactate, systemic features, peritonism

Herniated bowel or omentum with a compromised blood supply — the constricting neck of the hernia cuts off venous then arterial flow, leading to ischaemia and, within hours, infarction. A surgical emergency: bowel necrosis occurs quickly, so any irreducible, tender hernia needs SpR review at the time of assessment.

H History
  • Femoral hernia: lies below and lateral to the pubic tubercle (contrast: inguinal is above and medial) — easily missed in elderly women, highest strangulation risk
  • Painful irreducible lump at hernia site — constant severe pain, not colicky
  • Vomiting, absolute constipation if associated bowel obstruction
  • Previous hernia, previous repair, prior episodes of irreducibility
  • Known femoral hernia? — these have the highest strangulation risk
  • Onset and duration — ischaemia worsens with time

  • Temperature, HR, BP, RR — fever, tachycardia = systemic ischaemia
  • Identify hernia site:
  • Irreducible lump — no cough impulse. Compare: reducible (soft, goes back) vs strangulated (firm, tender, won't reduce).
  • Skin overlying hernia — red, warm, oedematous = late sign
  • Peritonism elsewhere = bowel perforation within sac → immediate

  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, LFTs, CRP, clotting, G&S. VBG — lactate.

M Management — F1 Actions
  1. IV analgesia, IV fluids, anti-emetics. NBM.
  2. Manage by risk tier:
    • 🟢 Irreducible — analgesia, then a single gentle reduction attempt by a senior if appropriate. Discuss with SpR.
    • 🟡 Obstructed — NBM, NG, IV fluids, catheter + fluid balance, CT, urgent surgical review.
    • 🔴 Ischaemic — do NOT reduce. Resuscitate, sepsis management, SpR + theatre. Emergency.
  3. Monitor observations closely.
  4. Antibiotics if strangulation confirmed, systemic features, sepsis, peritonism, or bowel resection anticipated — follow local intra-abdominal sepsis UHDB guideline.
  5. Give adequate analgesia first (IV morphine). With analgesia and muscle relaxation, an incarcerated hernia (no peritonism / not septic) often reduces spontaneously or with gentle manual pressure.
  6. Do NOT attempt reduction if peritonism, sepsis, or skin necrosis — this is strangulation. Call SpR first.

  • Prepare for emergency theatre: cross-match 2 units, alert anaesthetics and theatre
  • Never reduce a truly strangulated hernia — compromised bowel can be reduced back into the abdomen, masking the problem until peritonitis develops.

  • Obturator sign: pain on internal hip rotation — pelvic appendix

  1. NBM
  2. Analgesia: IV paracetamol + morphine — do NOT withhold. Evidence: analgesia does not mask peritonism.
  3. Anti-emetics
  4. If sepsis features → Sepsis 6 immediately
  5. Antibiotics: only start if a SpR instructs or there is imaging-proven appendicitis. Then per local intra-abdominal sepsis/appendicitis UHDB guideline, especially if perforated, septic, or going to theatre. Blood cultures only if complicated, septic, or septic shock suspected.

  • Review Alvarado score and imaging to plan: laparoscopic appendicectomy (gold standard, aim within 24hrs) vs non-operative management in selected uncomplicated cases — senior shared decision-making with patient counselling on recurrence risk

  • Anticoagulation uncovers pathology — it does not cause it. Always investigate.

  • The 6 Ps:

  • Pallor — white or mottled

  • Paralysis — motor deficit (LATE sign = emergency)

  • Recent trauma or heavy lifting?
E Examination
  • BP differential between arms >20 mmHg — key sign
  • Absent or reduced peripheral pulses — note distribution carefully
  • Lower limb neurological signs (spinal cord ischaemia)
  • Abdominal/groin pain — mesenteric or renal artery involvement
  • Cardiac auscultation: new aortic regurgitation murmur
  • Document a full set of peripheral pulses + neurology baseline
I Investigations
  • ECG — rule out STEMI (dissection can mimic MI)
  • CXR — widened mediastinum, but normal CXR does NOT exclude dissection
  • Troponin
  • CT aorta with contrast — definitive investigation
  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, CRP, clotting, G&S
  • VBG — lactate, K⁺
⚡Management — F1 Actions
  1. Lower BP — target SBP 100–120 mmHg. IV labetalol or esmolol first-line.
  2. HR control — target <60 bpm. Beta blockade.
  3. G&S, IV access (two large-bore), NBM.
  4. CT aorta with contrast — definitive imaging.
  5. Call vascular SpR (Type B) or cardiothoracic (Type A) immediately.
  6. Transfer to CCU for monitoring if uncomplicated Type B.
👨‍⚕️Seniors Will
  • No limb or visceral compromise → medical management in CCU — monitor BP and HR.
  • Repeat CT in 3 days if stable and not progressing.
  • If still stable: discharge with CT scheduled at 1 month and 1 year.
  • If surgery required: transfer for surgical management — Derby → Nottingham; Chesterfield → Sheffield.
  • Type A (ascending) → cardiothoracic surgery (out of vascular scope).

🚨 Escalate Immediately If

  • Limb ischaemia
  • Visceral organ compromise (mesenteric, renal)
  • Haemodynamic instability
  • Type A (ascending) — cardiothoracic emergency

💡 Pearls

  • NELA score for any patient going to emergency abdominal surgery — calculate at nela-risk-tool.apps.crownaudit.org before theatre booking.
  • Always check BP in BOTH arms — >20 mmHg differential is a key sign
  • Normal CXR does NOT exclude dissection — get the CT if suspicion is real
  • Dissection can mimic MI on ECG and troponin — clinical pattern is the giveaway

When to call SpR

Limb-threatening infection in a diabetic foot.
Time-critical — sepsis + ischaemia + tissue loss combine to threaten the limb.
If patient is known to the diabetic foot team, contact them in clinic hours.

H History
  • Known diabetic? Type 1 vs 2. Duration. Glycaemic control (last HbA1c)?
  • Existing diabetic foot care plan? Known to podiatry / diabetic foot team?
  • Time course: swelling, discharge, pain, fever — over what duration?
  • Last podiatry contact / footwear / pressure points?
  • Smoker? Peripheral vascular disease history?
  • Previous amputation or revascularisation?
E Examination
  • Perfusion: dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial pulses. Capillary refill. Temperature gradient up the limb.
  • Probe-to-bone test — if probe reaches bone, treat as osteomyelitis.
  • Document margins of erythema, depth of ulcer, exposed tissue.
  • Crepitus / spreading erythema / fluctuance — necrotising soft tissue infection.
  • Wet gangrene (boggy, malodorous) vs dry gangrene (mummified, demarcated).
  • Systemic features: pyrexia, tachycardia, hypotension.
I Investigations
  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, CRP, clotting, G&S, blood glucose, HbA1c, lactate.
  • Blood cultures ×2 from separate sites before starting antibiotics.
  • ECG — pre-theatre baseline.
  • X-ray foot — look for osteomyelitis (cortical erosion) or gas in soft tissues.
  • Deep wound swab MC&S — not superficial; reflects organism colonising the deep tissue.
  • Consider MRI foot if osteomyelitis suspected and X-ray negative.
⚡Management — F1 Actions
  1. Assess against known diabetic foot team plan (if patient known).
  2. IV access × 2 large bore, NBM (theatre may be needed), IV fluids.
  3. Blood cultures ×2 before antibiotics.
  4. IV antibiotics as per UHDB diabetic foot protocol — check NETI.
  5. Paracetamol for pyrexia.
  6. Glycaemic control — variable rate insulin if needed; involve diabetes team.
  7. Call vascular SpR — always.
👨‍⚕️Seniors Will
  • Theatre likely — urgent debridement ± toe/foot amputation. Consultant-led decision.
  • Involve diabetes team for glycaemic control and ongoing management.
  • Plan revascularisation if perfusion is poor (angiogram + intervention).

🚨 Escalate Immediately If

  • Sepsis features
  • Crepitus or gas on X-ray — possible necrotising fasciitis (Fournier's-like emergency)
  • Wet gangrene
  • Rapid clinical deterioration

💡 Pearls

  • NELA score for any patient going to emergency abdominal surgery — calculate at nela-risk-tool.apps.crownaudit.org before theatre booking.
  • Probe-to-bone test: if positive → osteomyelitis until proven otherwise.
  • Wet gangrene = surgical emergency. Dry gangrene = often a conservative plan with demarcation and elective amputation.
  • Deep swab, not superficial — superficial swabs grow colonising organisms only.

📝Key Rules
  • Blood cultures ×2 — from 2 separate venepuncture sites, 1–15 minutes apart — always before starting antibiotics.
  • Duration (peritonitis): generally 4 days from surgical intervention and source control.
  • Antifungals: reserved for Candida on cultures or immunocompromise — not routine.
  • Vancomycin targets: pre-dose 15–20 mg/L. Teicoplanin: pre-dose 15–30 mg/L.
  • Always de-escalate per sensitivities once cultures return (NETI).
  • Hospital-acquired intra-abdominal abscess: pip-tazo QDS (every 6 hours — not 8 hourly).
